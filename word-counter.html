    How to use the word counter

    1. Type or paste your text into the editor. Counts update automatically after a short pause, so long documents stay responsive.
    2. Read the quick counts for words, characters, sentences and paragraphs. The detailed table below them adds characters without spaces, unique words, syllables and more.
    3. Check readability if you are writing English prose. The three scores estimate how hard the text is to read, not whether it is good.
    4. Review keyword density to see which words and phrases repeat. It is a diagnostic, not a target to hit.
    5. Compare against platform limits before publishing a post, meta description or SMS message.
    6. Copy the report or download a CSV if you need the numbers outside the page.

    The tool works offline after the page loads. No text is sent to a server, and nothing is stored after you close or reload the tab.

    How reading time is estimated

    Reading time is words divided by reading speed. The default is 200 words per minute, the same baseline used by Medium, DEV.to and most reading-time plugins[reference:2]. You can switch to 150, 250 or 300 words per minute in the tool.

    Speaking time uses 130 words per minute, a typical presentation or voice-over pace. Handwriting uses 20 words per minute. These are planning estimates. Dense technical text is usually read 30–50% slower, and a rehearsed speech is often delivered faster than a cold read[reference:3].

    Understanding the readability scores

    All three scores use word length and sentence length as proxies for difficulty. None of them understands meaning, so a short sentence with difficult ideas can score as “easy” while a clear sentence with a long word can score as “hard”.

    How to interpret readability scores
    ScoreWhat it measuresHow to read it
    Flesch Reading EaseSentence length and syllables per word0–100, higher is easier. 60–70 is plain English; below 50 is difficult for a general audience.
    Flesch–Kincaid GradeThe same inputs, expressed as a US school gradeA score of 8 means a typical eighth-grade reader should manage it. Most web content aims for 7–9.
    Gunning FogSentence length and the share of words with three or more syllablesEstimates years of formal education needed. Lower is easier; technical writing often lands higher.

    Scores are shown only when the text has at least 20 words, at least one sentence, and is mostly written in Latin script. Very short text produces unreliable numbers, so the tool shows a dash instead of a misleading score.

    Keyword density: use it as a diagnostic

    Keyword density is the number of times a word or phrase appears divided by the total number of words. It is useful for spotting accidental repetition — for example, using content twelve times in a 400-word page. It is not a ranking lever.

    There is no correct density to target. Older advice suggested 1–3% for a main keyword, but modern search systems evaluate meaning and usefulness, and repeating a phrase to hit a percentage makes writing worse[reference:4]. Treat a high density as a prompt to reread the paragraph, not as a number to optimise.

    Two-word and three-word phrases are often more informative than single words. Credit card is a meaningful term; credit and card counted separately are not. The phrase tables show terms that single-word analysis misses[reference:5].

    Limitations to know

    • Word boundaries differ between tools. This tool counts hyphenated compounds as one word, matching common word processors. Some tools split them, so counts can differ by a few percent.
    • Sentence detection is punctuation-based. Abbreviations are protected, but unusual punctuation, missing full stops or text without sentence endings can produce a different sentence count than a human would.
    • Readability scores are English-only estimates. They are hidden for short text and for text that is mostly non-Latin script.
    • Platform limits are approximate. X applies its own weighting, and SMS depends on the character encoding. Always confirm in the platform’s own composer before publishing.
    • CJK and Thai word counts depend on the browser. When the browser provides Intl.Segmenter, those languages are segmented into real words. Older browsers may fall back to character-based counting.
    • Emoji counts can differ by definition. “Visible characters” counts grapheme clusters, which is what a person sees. “Characters (with spaces)” counts UTF-16 code units, which is what many systems store.
    • The page does not save your text. Reloading or closing the tab clears it. Copy anything you need before leaving.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is the word count calculated?

    The tool extracts sequences of letters and numbers, keeping apostrophes and internal hyphens. don't and state-of-the-art each count as one word. For Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Thai, the browser’s word segmenter is used so a sentence is not counted as a single word.

    Does punctuation count as a word?

    No. Punctuation alone is not a word. A token must contain at least one letter or number. Dashes, bullets and symbols are ignored.

    Why do character counts differ from other tools?

    There are three common definitions: UTF-16 code units (string.length), code points, and visible characters. An emoji can be one visible character but several code units. This tool shows all three so you can match whichever limit you are checking.

    How is reading time estimated?

    Words divided by reading speed. The default is 200 words per minute; you can switch to 150, 250 or 300. Speaking time uses 130 words per minute and handwriting uses 20. These are estimates, and technical text is usually read more slowly.

    Why is my readability score blank?

    Scores need at least 20 words, at least one sentence, and mostly Latin-script text. Short samples, lists, code and non-English scripts do not produce reliable scores, so the tool shows a dash rather than a misleading number.

    Is keyword density a ranking factor?

    Not in the way older SEO advice suggested. Density is a diagnostic for spotting repetition. Repeating a keyword to reach a percentage makes writing worse. Use the phrase tables to check that the terms you care about appear naturally.

    Does it count Chinese, Japanese or Korean words correctly?

    Yes, in browsers that support Intl.Segmenter. Those languages are written without spaces, so the tool uses the browser’s dictionary-based segmentation instead of splitting on spaces. In older browsers, the fallback may count characters rather than words.

    Can I check X, LinkedIn or meta description limits?

    Yes. The platform limits section shows visible-character counts against X (280), Bluesky (300), Threads (500), Instagram (2,200), LinkedIn (3,000), SMS (160), meta title (60) and meta description (160). X applies its own weighting, so confirm in the composer before publishing.

    Is my text uploaded or stored?

    No. All counting, scoring and keyword analysis runs in the browser. No request carries your text anywhere. When you close or reload the tab, the text is gone unless you copied or downloaded it.

    What is the difference between characters with and without spaces?

    “With spaces” is the raw length including whitespace, which is what most form fields measure. “Without spaces” strips whitespace first, which is useful for limits that exclude spaces. The visible-character count is the one to use for social platforms.
